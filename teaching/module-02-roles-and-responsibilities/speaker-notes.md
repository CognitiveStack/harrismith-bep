**Status:** Working notes. **Not a script, and not final.**

These support speaking in your own words. The *essential message* must be

## Slide 1 — BIM roles exist to make decisions explicit · 2.0 min

### Purpose

Establish why role clarity matters before naming a single role — and show that
unassigned authority produces an accidental decision rather than no decision.

### Essential message

1. Multidisciplinary delivery contains **decisions**, not just work, and they sit
   at the **interfaces** between teams.
2. Those decisions get made either way.
3. Unassigned, they get made by **assumption, access or habit**.
4. Deliberate assignment means: for any container, it is answerable **who
   produced it, who checked it, who authorised it, and for what purpose**.

### Suggested wording

> "Before I name a single role, I want to say why this matters at all.
>
> A multidisciplinary project isn't just a lot of work happening in parallel.
> It's a series of decisions — mostly small ones, mostly at the edges where one
> team's information meets another's. Is this ready to share? Has anyone checked
> it? Can we rely on it? Who says so?
>
> Here's the thing. Those decisions get made. Every time. The only question is
> *how*.
>
> If nobody's been assigned, they get made three ways. By **assumption** —
> the architect usually leads that sort of thing, doesn't he. By **access** — I
> could write to that folder, so I did. Or by **habit** — that's how we did it
> last time.
>
> None of those is a decision anyone took. They're all decisions that happened.
>
> The Harrismith BEP puts the alternative in one sentence: for any information
> container, it must be answerable who produced it, who checked it, who
> authorised it, and for what purpose. Four questions. If you can answer them,
> you have roles. If you can't, you have habits."

### Shorter fallback

> "A multidisciplinary project is full of decisions at the interfaces — is this
> ready, has it been checked, can we rely on it. Those decisions get made either
> way. Unassigned, they get made by assumption, by whoever has access, or by
> habit. Assigned, you can answer four questions about any piece of information:
> who produced it, who checked it, who authorised it, and for what purpose."

### Harrismith evidence

Three separate sources close the three accidental routes:

- **Assumption** — *"Architecture is not automatically the Lead Delivery
  Party."* That Architecture was the only populated production stream observed is
  an observation about files, not an appointment.
- **Access** — *"Authority is never inferred upward from platform
  configuration."*
- **Habit** — reconfiguring a team, space, permission or mapping *"does not
  constitute a governance decision, and does not make itself legitimate by having
  been done."*

### Likely question

"Isn't this just organisation charts?"

### Safe answer

No — and the BEP is explicit that its role model is a *conceptual functional
model, not an appointment chart and not an organisation chart*. An org chart says
who reports to whom. This says who is permitted to decide what. On a project with
several organisations, the second matters more and is written down less.

### Transition to Slide 2

"So — roles. And straight away there's a confusion worth clearing, because
'role' and 'job title' are not the same thing."

### Delivery warning

**The likely misunderstanding: the audience hears the three accidental routes as
an accusation about their own projects — or worse, about Harrismith.**

Two guards. Keep the tone descriptive rather than critical: these are conditions,
not misconduct, and everyone in the room has worked this way. And **do not
present any of it as a recorded Harrismith failure** — the sources record
observations, not failures. If asked whether this happened on Harrismith, the
honest answer is that the repository records one mapping discrepancy and no
failures at all.

### Evidence classification

**DIRECT** — the traceability requirement; the known-originator aim; the three
sourced closures; authority not inferred from configuration.
**INTERP** — decisions sitting at interfaces.
**SYNTH** — the central message. Teaching wording.
**EXCLUDED** — any generic problem attributed to Harrismith as history.

## Slide 2 — A role is not the same as a job title · 1.0 min

### Purpose

Separate the function from the label, and locate authority in the project's
agreed arrangements rather than in the title.

### Essential message

1. **"These are functions, not job titles and not people."**
2. One participant may carry several; **combining them does not merge them**.
3. The participant must know **which function they are performing at each
   decision point**.
4. **Delegation must be explicit** — and **platform access is not delegation**.

### Suggested wording

> "A role here isn't a job title. The BEP says it directly: these are functions,
> not job titles and not people.
>
> That matters because one person can carry several. On a project this size,
> probably will. And here's the part that gets missed — carrying two functions
> doesn't merge them.
>
> The BEP has a lovely example of this. Approving a governance change as BIM
> Manager is a different act from applying it as CDE Administrator — even when
> it's the same person, doing both, within a minute of each other. Same human.
> Two functions. Two different authorities. And they have to know which one
> they're exercising.
>
> Which brings me to the thing that isn't a role: your job title. Being called a
> BIM Manager doesn't tell you what you're allowed to decide on this project.
> That comes from what the project agreed. And there's a specific version of this
> the BEP is firm about — the BIM Manager is not automatically the appointing
> party, and not automatically the lead delivery party. Different functions,
> different concerns, and they may sit in different organisations entirely."

### Shorter fallback

> "A role is a project function, not a job title. One person can carry several,
> and carrying two doesn't merge them — approving a change as BIM Manager is a
> different act from applying it as CDE Administrator, even a minute apart. Your
> title doesn't tell you what you can decide here; the project's arrangements
> do."

### Harrismith evidence

BEP §4.6, verbatim: *"These are functions, not job titles and not people."*

BEP §5.11's one-person-two-functions example, above — the whole slide in one
sentence.

BEP §5.11 on delegation: it *"must be explicit"* — stated, scoped and recorded —
and **"platform access is not delegation."** Being able to perform an action in
the software does not mean authority to decide it was delegated.

### Likely question

"So who *is* the BIM Manager on Harrismith?"

### Safe answer

Nobody. Every role holder is TBD — no appointing party, no lead delivery party,
no BIM manager, no coordinator, no task-team leads. Harrismith defines functions
and allocates them; it names no one. Slide 3 is about why that is deliberate
rather than unfinished.

### Transition to Slide 3

"So a function isn't a title. It's also not an organisation, and it's not a
person — and those three are worth pulling apart properly."

### Delivery warning

**The likely misunderstanding: someone in the room whose title is "BIM Manager"
hears this as their authority being reduced.**

Depersonalise it. The point is not that the function holds less — it is that the
function holds *something specific*, and specificity protects the holder as much
as it constrains them. A BIM Manager who is not the design approver cannot be
blamed for a design decision. Say that if the temperature rises.

**Second risk:** do not let this drift into Slides 5–6. The BIM Manager's
boundaries get fifteen minutes' worth of attention later; here they are only an
illustration that titles do not confer authority.

### Evidence classification

**DIRECT** — the functions-not-titles statement; combining does not merge; the
one-person-two-functions example; explicit delegation; access is not delegation;
the BIM Manager / AP / LDP boundaries.
**INTERP** — authority comes from the project's arrangements, not the title.
**SYNTH** — the same title carrying different authority on different projects.
**EXCLUDED** — any named person.

## Slide 3 — Function, organisation and named person · 1.0 min

### Purpose

Show three layers, and explain why Harrismith populates two and deliberately
leaves the third empty.

### Essential message

1. Three layers: **function** (what must be decided) → **organisation or party**
   (who is responsible) → **named role holder** (who actually does it).
2. Harrismith establishes the first, defines categories at the second, and leaves
   the third **TBD throughout**.
3. **That is a planning state, not a defect** — but implementation eventually
   requires names.
4. **A mapping is not an identity.** Party, task team, discipline, platform team
   and IM role are five different things.

### Suggested wording

> "Three layers, and they get flattened constantly.
>
> First: the **function**. What has to be done or decided. Authoring. Checking.
> Authorising a share. Coordinating an interface.
>
> Second: the **organisation** — which party is responsible for that function.
> Third: the **named person** who actually performs it.
>
> Harrismith does the first two and stops. There are four information-management
> functions defined at organisation level, there are nine functional roles in the
> responsibility matrix, and every single holder is marked TBD. The BEP says it
> in four words: no names are populated.
>
> Now — is that a problem? Not yet, and I want to be careful here. You can define
> functions, allocate them and review them before anybody's appointed. That's
> useful, because a BEP gets written when the team is still forming. What you
> *can't* do is run a project on it. Implementation eventually needs names, and
> Harrismith records that it hasn't got there.
>
> One more thing, because it's the sharpest line in this section. The BEP lists
> five concepts that are deliberately not interchangeable — party, task team,
> discipline, Autodesk collaboration team, and information-management role. They
> often map onto each other. But — and this is the quote — a mapping is not an
> identity. Each one is a decision to be recorded, not an assumption to inherit."

### Shorter fallback

> "Three layers: the function, the organisation responsible for it, and the
> person who performs it. Harrismith defines the functions and the party
> categories, and leaves every named holder TBD. That's a planning state, not a
> defect — but implementation eventually needs names. And party, task team,
> discipline, platform team and IM role are five different things: they often map
> onto each other, but a mapping is not an identity."

### Harrismith evidence

BEP §4.6 — **four** IM functions at organisation level: BIM Manager, BIM
Coordinator, CDE Administration, Lead Delivery Party.

BEP §5.2 — *"No names are populated. Role holders are TBD throughout Section 5"*,
and the model is *"a conceptual functional model, not an appointment chart and
not an organisation chart."*

IM matrix §5, the caution worth carrying: allocating functions to roles *"does
not demonstrate that separate people perform them, and it must not be read as
evidence of independence."*

### Likely question

"Isn't a framework with no names just theoretical?"

### Safe answer

It's incomplete, and it says so. But the sequence is right — you agree what has
to be decided before you argue about who decides it, or the argument is about
people rather than process. What Harrismith can't do is *operate*. Implementation
needs holders, and the repository records that it hasn't reached that point.

### Transition to Slide 4

"So those are the layers. Let's populate the middle one — which functions govern
project information, and how they relate."

### Delivery warning

**The likely misunderstanding: the audience concludes Harrismith is unfinished
and mentally discounts the rest of the talk.**

This is the highest-risk moment in Slides 1–3. You have just said every holder is
TBD, eleven slides before you get to explain why that is discipline rather than
deficiency.

Get ahead of it in the same breath: say **why** functions-before-names is the
right order, and say plainly what it cannot do. An audience given both halves
accepts it. An audience given only the first half concludes the framework is
theoretical, and Slide 13 will not recover them.

**Second risk:** do not say "five functions". The source's organisation-level
list has **four**. The five-act framing belongs to Module 1 and is teaching
structure — see [`source-map.md`](source-map.md) §5.

### Evidence classification

**DIRECT** — the five non-interchangeable concepts and "a mapping is not an
identity"; the four IM functions; "no names are populated"; the conceptual-model
statement; the matrix's independence caution.
**INTERP** — the three-layer framing itself.
**SYNTH** — the argument that assigning functions before names is useful during
planning.
**EXCLUDED** — that TBD holders make the framework invalid.

## Notes on using these notes

- **Slide 1 is the one to rehearse.** If the audience does not accept that
  unassigned authority produces accidental decisions, the remaining thirteen
  slides are a taxonomy exercise.
- **Slide 3's delivery warning is not optional.** Both halves of the TBD argument
  must be delivered together.
- **Two recurring answers to have ready**, because they arrive throughout:
  *every role holder is TBD*, and *four authorities remain unresolved*. Say both
  as ordinary facts about a training implementation.
- **Never say "lead appointed party" or "appointed party."** Those terms appear
  nowhere in the Harrismith sources. Harrismith uses **Owner / Appointing Party**
  and **Lead Delivery Party**.
- **Never introduce RACI.** Expressly not adopted, in two sources, for the same
  stated reason.
- Speaker notes for Slides 4–14 do not exist yet.
