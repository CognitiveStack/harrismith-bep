# Module 3 — Exercises

**Status:** Initial exercise set for the Slides 1–3 baseline. Teaching material;
**not governance.**

Rehearsal exercises for the presenter, not assessments for an audience. Do them
aloud, alone, before delivering to anyone. **Exercise 10 is done after each of
the others**, and it is the one that matters most in this module.

**Four exercises rehearse questions rather than slides.** Exercises 5–9 cover
material whose slides are not yet developed — but the questions arrive the moment
you say the words "ISO 19650" in a room. Rehearse the answers before the slides
exist, not after.

---

## Exercise 1 — Explain ISO 19650 without mentioning software

**Task.** Explain what ISO 19650 is, in under a minute, **without naming any
software** — no Revit, no ACC, no Navisworks, no BIM 360, no "the platform", no
"the system", no "the tool".

**Why this exercise.** Software is the audience's default frame, and it is
contagious. If the first tool word comes from you, every subsequent sentence is
heard as a software feature — and Slide 2 then has to fight your own Slide 1.

**What good looks like.** You describe information: what a project needs to make
decisions, how it is specified before it is produced, how it is managed and
exchanged, how it is recorded so it can be relied on later, and that this runs
across an asset's life. You stay inside published scope and you flag your own
framing as yours.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] Not one software or platform word passed my lips.
- [ ] I described **information for decisions**, not information for filing.
- [ ] I said something about the **life cycle**, not just design and build.
- [ ] I labelled at least one sentence as my own wording, not the standard's.
- [ ] Under sixty seconds.

**Common failure.** Substituting "the environment" or "the CDE" and continuing.
Same frame, different noun. A CDE is a process — but the audience hears the
software they were sold.

## Exercise 2 — Information versus a model

**Task.** Explain the difference between **information** and **a model**. Forty-
five seconds. Use one concrete example.

**Why this exercise.** The audience's mental image of BIM is a 3D model. If
"information" and "model" stay fused, the whole framework collapses into
modelling practice — and Slides 5, 6 and 9 become unteachable.

**What good looks like.** A model is one container of information among many. The
information is what someone needs in order to decide something: a dimension, a
status, a clearance, a responsible party, a date. A drawing, a schedule, a report
and a model can all carry it. The model is a vehicle; the information is the
cargo — and the decision is why either exists.

**Reference.** Harrismith's own framing is useful and is `HARRISMITH` class: BEP
§1.1 aims for every **information container** to have a known originator, a known
state, a known route to the next state, and a known role accountable for moving
it. Note the word: *container*, not *model*.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I distinguished the **carrier** from the **content**.
- [ ] I gave exactly one concrete example and stayed with it.
- [ ] I mentioned at least one non-model container.
- [ ] I connected information to a **decision** someone has to make.
- [ ] I did not claim this is ISO's definition — because I do not have it.

**Common failure.** "Information is the data in the model." That makes the model
primary and everything else a by-product, which is backwards for the module.

## Exercise 3 — Why platform access does not prove compliance

**Task.** Someone says: *"We've got the platform configured properly, so we're
compliant."* Answer in thirty seconds without being rude and without hedging.

**Why this exercise.** It is Slide 2's whole content and the most likely challenge
from a technically confident audience member. Hedged answers ("well, it certainly
helps…") are heard as agreement.

**What good looks like.** Configuration is not conformity. Permissions describe
what the software allows, not what someone is permitted to decide. Where a file
sits does not establish its status — state, version, revision, status and
suitability are five separate properties. And our own validation record says it
about us: **folder names do not themselves prove ISO 19650 governance.**

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I said **no** clearly, in the first sentence.
- [ ] I separated **permission** from **authority**.
- [ ] I made the point that location does not establish status.
- [ ] I attributed the five-properties point to **our BEP**, not to ISO.
- [ ] I did not soften it into a yes at the end.

**Common failure.** The trailing softener — "…but you're basically most of the way
there." You have just conceded the point you spent thirty seconds refuting.

## Exercise 4 — Part 1 against Part 2, in sixty seconds

**Task.** Compare `ISO 19650-1:2018` and `ISO 19650-2:2018`. **Exactly sixty
seconds.** Time it.

**Why this exercise.** It is Slide 3's full allocation, so this rehearsal is the
slide. If it takes ninety seconds here, it will take two minutes live and Slide 13
pays.

**What good looks like.** Part 1: concepts and principles, the framework,
exchanging, recording, versioning and organising information, whole asset life
cycle. Part 2: an information-management process for the delivery phase and the
exchanges within it, applicable across asset types, organisations and procurement
routes. Then the one-liner: Part 1 explains the framework; Part 2 applies a
process to delivery. Then the revision warning, in one sentence.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] Both editions named as **2018**.
- [ ] Life-cycle reach on Part 1; **delivery phase** on Part 2.
- [ ] The one-line summary said, and labelled as my interpretation.
- [ ] The revision warning included — and **no draft content described**.
- [ ] Sixty seconds or under, measured.
- [ ] **Zero clause numbers.** Not one.

**Common failure.** Padding the comparison with invented detail because four
bullets per side feels thin. There is no more detail available; anything added is
fabrication.

## Exercise 5 — Why a draft is not a current requirement

**Task.** Someone says: *"I've seen the draft of the new Part 1 — shouldn't we be
working to that?"* Answer in thirty seconds.

**Why this exercise.** It will happen. Someone in a mixed audience follows the
committees, and answering badly either fabricates content or concedes that drafts
govern.

**What good looks like.** A draft is a proposal. It can change before it
publishes, and it can fail to publish. What is published is what is current, and
that is the 2018 editions. Recording that a revision exists is sensible; teaching
from it is not. And then the honest half: *I don't know what's in it.*

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I said clearly that the 2018 editions remain current.
- [ ] I explained **why** — a draft is a proposal and may change.
- [ ] I did not describe, guess at or characterise any draft content.
- [ ] I did not invent a stage code or a publication date.
- [ ] I said "I don't know" where I don't — without embarrassment.

**Common failure.** Guessing at a timeline. "Probably next year" is a fabrication
with a friendly face, and the room will remember it.

## Exercise 6 — ISO against UK implementation guidance

**Task.** Explain the difference between ISO 19650 and the UK BIM Framework.
Forty-five seconds. Then state what it means for **a South African project**.

**Why this exercise.** The guidance is the most usable material available and the
most easily mistaken for the standard. The second half is the part presenters
skip, and it is the part that matters here.

**What good looks like.** ISO 19650 is the international standard. The UK BIM
Framework is **official implementation guidance for the UK** — useful, worth
reading, and not the standard's text. It is not a universal national annex, and
it is not automatically applicable in South Africa. Then the honest close: no
South African implementation guidance is registered for this programme, and
whether an applicable framework exists is an **open question**, not something to
fill in with UK material.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I named it as **guidance**, not as the standard.
- [ ] I said **UK** out loud.
- [ ] I said it is **not automatically applicable** here.
- [ ] I stated the South African position as **open**, not as absent-therefore-UK.
- [ ] I did not disparage the guidance — it is good material, correctly labelled.

**Common failure.** "It's basically the same thing, just explained better." That
is exactly the substitution the source hierarchy exists to prevent.

## Exercise 7 — Identify one Harrismith analogue and label it correctly

**Task.** Pick **one** Harrismith arrangement, explain what ISO-associated
principle it illustrates, and **say its mapping category out loud**. Sixty
seconds.

**Why this exercise.** Slide 13 is the module's honesty slide, and it fails
quietly — not by claiming compliance outright, but by describing an analogue in
language that sounds like equivalence.

**What good looks like.** Take the CDE state set — WIP, Shared, Published /
Authorised, Record / Retained. Explain that it illustrates controlled progression
and purposeful sharing. Then label it: **`LOCAL ANALOGUE OR INTERPRETATION`** — it
**resembles** ISO-associated practice, and conformity **has not been
demonstrated**. Note in passing that we say *Record / Retained*, not *Archived*.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I chose exactly one arrangement.
- [ ] I named the category **aloud**, in the four-category vocabulary.
- [ ] I used the word **resembles** or **illustrates** — never *complies* or
      *matches*.
- [ ] I used Harrismith's own term, unaltered.
- [ ] I did not claim the standard requires what we do.

**Common failure.** "This is our implementation of the ISO state model." Two
overclaims in eight words: that there is an ISO state model we have seen, and
that ours implements it.

## Exercise 8 — Identify one Harrismith gap or unverified matter

**Task.** Name one thing about Harrismith that **cannot** be mapped to ISO 19650
from available material, and explain what would be needed to resolve it. Forty-
five seconds.

**Why this exercise.** A presenter who only shows analogues has given a compliance
talk with cautious adjectives. The gaps are what make the mapping credible.

**What good looks like.** Any of these:

| Gap | What would resolve it |
|---|---|
| `Lead Delivery Party` against *lead appointed party* | A licensed copy of the standard, plus a recorded mapping decision |
| *appointed party* — **absent from our sources entirely** | The same |
| Whether our state model corresponds to the standard's | A licensed copy; a conformity assessment |
| Whether any South African implementation guidance applies | External research — not done |
| Whether we meet any requirement at all | **A clause-by-clause assessment that does not exist** |

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I named a **specific** gap, not "we've got gaps".
- [ ] I said what would be needed to resolve it.
- [ ] I did not resolve it by guessing.
- [ ] I did not present the gap as a failure — it is an unfinished mapping.
- [ ] I resisted substituting the ISO-associated term for ours "just to explain
      it".

**Common failure.** "Lead Delivery Party — that's basically the lead appointed
party." That is the exact substitution Module 2 recorded and deferred, made
casually, in an aside, where nobody writes it down.

## Exercise 9 — "Are we ISO-compliant?"

**Task.** Answer it. Thirty seconds. No notes.

**Why this exercise.** It is the question. It will be asked by whoever has the
most authority in the room, and it is usually asked hoping for a yes.

**What good looks like.**

> "No — and we've never claimed to be. Our BEP says it in writing: ISO 19650
> principles inform how we manage information, and that's a statement about
> influence. No compliance is claimed, and none has been assessed. There's no
> clause-by-clause assessment anywhere in this project, and there couldn't be —
> nobody here has worked through the standard against what we do. What we do have
> is a documented information-management approach that shows the principles
> working, which is a different and more useful thing for a training
> implementation."

**Success criteria**

- [ ] **No** was the first word, or near enough.
- [ ] I cited the **BEP's own statement**, not my own caution.
- [ ] I said no clause-by-clause assessment exists.
- [ ] I did not offer "we're probably close" or "we'd pass if assessed".
- [ ] I gave the positive close — influence and illustration — without
      retracting the no.
- [ ] I did not blame anyone for the absence. It is a deliberate position.

**Common failure.** "Not formally, no." *Formally* implies informal compliance
exists. It does not. There is no informal conformity, and the qualifier gives the
room the yes they wanted.

## Exercise 10 — Self-assessment: did I keep the three apart?

**Do this after every other exercise, and after every rehearsal of every slide.**

**Task.** Replay what you just said. For each substantive statement, name which
of three it was:

| | Class | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | **Principle** — published ISO scope | Would this survive someone reading `X1`/`X2`'s published scope? |
| **G** | **Guidance** — official implementation guidance | Did I say **UK** when I used it? |
| **E** | **Project evidence** — Harrismith | Did I attribute it to our documents rather than to the standard? |

Then the residue: anything that was **your own framing** — interpretation or
synthesis. **That is fine, provided you knew it at the time.**

**Success criteria**

- [ ] Every substantive statement got a letter — none was unclassifiable.
- [ ] No **E** statement was delivered in language that made it sound like **P**.
- [ ] Every **G** statement carried the word *UK*.
- [ ] No **P** statement went beyond published scope into requirement.
- [ ] My own framing was flagged **at the time**, not reconstructed afterwards.
- [ ] I said "I don't know" at least once where I genuinely did not.

**Common failure — and the one this whole module is built to prevent.** Delivering
a Harrismith statement with the confidence of a standard. It never sounds like a
lie. It sounds like expertise. That is exactly why it needs a checklist.

**If you cannot classify a sentence, do not say it.** Rewrite it until you can, or
drop it.
