# Module 3 — Exercises

**Status:** Exercise set for the **Slides 1–12** baseline. Teaching material;
**not governance.**

Rehearsal exercises for the presenter, not assessments for an audience. Do them
aloud, alone, before delivering to anyone. **Exercise 10 is done after each of
the others**, and it is the one that matters most in this module.

**Exercises 1–10 cover Slides 1–3 and the questions that arrive regardless.
Exercises 11–22 cover Section B — Slides 4–6. Exercises 23–34 cover Section C —
Slides 7–9. Exercises 35–46 cover Section D and the Section E opener —
Slides 10–12.**

**Four exercises rehearse questions rather than slides.** Exercises 5–9 cover
material whose slides are not yet developed — but the questions arrive the moment
you say the words "ISO 19650" in a room. Rehearse the answers before the slides
exist, not after.

---

## Exercise 1 — Explain ISO 19650 without mentioning software

**Task.** Explain what ISO 19650 is, in under a minute, **without naming any
software** — no Revit, no ACC, no Navisworks, no BIM 360, no "the platform", no
"the system", no "the tool".

**Why this exercise.** Software is the audience's default frame, and it is
contagious. If the first tool word comes from you, every subsequent sentence is
heard as a software feature — and Slide 2 then has to fight your own Slide 1.

**What good looks like.** You describe information: what a project needs to make
decisions, how it is specified before it is produced, how it is managed and
exchanged, how it is recorded so it can be relied on later, and that this runs
across an asset's life. You stay inside published scope and you flag your own
framing as yours.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] Not one software or platform word passed my lips.
- [ ] I described **information for decisions**, not information for filing.
- [ ] I said something about the **life cycle**, not just design and build.
- [ ] I labelled at least one sentence as my own wording, not the standard's.
- [ ] Under sixty seconds.

**Common failure.** Substituting "the environment" or "the CDE" and continuing.
Same frame, different noun. A CDE is a process — but the audience hears the
software they were sold.

## Exercise 2 — Information versus a model

**Task.** Explain the difference between **information** and **a model**. Forty-
five seconds. Use one concrete example.

**Why this exercise.** The audience's mental image of BIM is a 3D model. If
"information" and "model" stay fused, the whole framework collapses into
modelling practice — and Slides 5, 6 and 9 become unteachable.

**What good looks like.** A model is one container of information among many. The
information is what someone needs in order to decide something: a dimension, a
status, a clearance, a responsible party, a date. A drawing, a schedule, a report
and a model can all carry it. The model is a vehicle; the information is the
cargo — and the decision is why either exists.

**Reference.** Harrismith's own framing is useful and is `HARRISMITH` class: BEP
§1.1 aims for every **information container** to have a known originator, a known
state, a known route to the next state, and a known role accountable for moving
it. Note the word: *container*, not *model*.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I distinguished the **carrier** from the **content**.
- [ ] I gave exactly one concrete example and stayed with it.
- [ ] I mentioned at least one non-model container.
- [ ] I connected information to a **decision** someone has to make.
- [ ] I did not claim this is ISO's definition — because I do not have it.

**Common failure.** "Information is the data in the model." That makes the model
primary and everything else a by-product, which is backwards for the module.

## Exercise 3 — Why platform access does not prove compliance

**Task.** Someone says: *"We've got the platform configured properly, so we're
compliant."* Answer in thirty seconds without being rude and without hedging.

**Why this exercise.** It is Slide 2's whole content and the most likely challenge
from a technically confident audience member. Hedged answers ("well, it certainly
helps…") are heard as agreement.

**What good looks like.** Configuration is not conformity. Permissions describe
what the software allows, not what someone is permitted to decide. Where a file
sits does not establish its status — state, version, revision, status and
suitability are five separate properties. And our own validation record says it
about us: **folder names do not themselves prove ISO 19650 governance.**

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I said **no** clearly, in the first sentence.
- [ ] I separated **permission** from **authority**.
- [ ] I made the point that location does not establish status.
- [ ] I attributed the five-properties point to **our BEP**, not to ISO.
- [ ] I did not soften it into a yes at the end.

**Common failure.** The trailing softener — "…but you're basically most of the way
there." You have just conceded the point you spent thirty seconds refuting.

## Exercise 4 — Part 1 against Part 2, in sixty seconds

**Task.** Compare `ISO 19650-1:2018` and `ISO 19650-2:2018`. **Exactly sixty
seconds.** Time it.

**Why this exercise.** It is Slide 3's full allocation, so this rehearsal is the
slide. If it takes ninety seconds here, it will take two minutes live and Slide 13
pays.

**What good looks like.** Part 1: concepts and principles, the framework,
exchanging, recording, versioning and organising information, whole asset life
cycle. Part 2: an information-management process for the delivery phase and the
exchanges within it, applicable across asset types, organisations and procurement
routes. Then the one-liner: Part 1 explains the framework; Part 2 applies a
process to delivery. Then the revision warning, in one sentence.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] Both editions named as **2018**.
- [ ] Life-cycle reach on Part 1; **delivery phase** on Part 2.
- [ ] The one-line summary said, and labelled as my interpretation.
- [ ] The revision warning included — and **no draft content described**.
- [ ] Sixty seconds or under, measured.
- [ ] **Zero clause numbers.** Not one.

**Common failure.** Padding the comparison with invented detail because four
bullets per side feels thin. There is no more detail available; anything added is
fabrication.

## Exercise 5 — Why a draft is not a current requirement

**Task.** Someone says: *"I've seen the draft of the new Part 1 — shouldn't we be
working to that?"* Answer in thirty seconds.

**Why this exercise.** It will happen. Someone in a mixed audience follows the
committees, and answering badly either fabricates content or concedes that drafts
govern.

**What good looks like.** A draft is a proposal. It can change before it
publishes, and it can fail to publish. What is published is what is current, and
that is the 2018 editions. Recording that a revision exists is sensible; teaching
from it is not. And then the honest half: *I don't know what's in it.*

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I said clearly that the 2018 editions remain current.
- [ ] I explained **why** — a draft is a proposal and may change.
- [ ] I did not describe, guess at or characterise any draft content.
- [ ] I did not invent a stage code or a publication date.
- [ ] I said "I don't know" where I don't — without embarrassment.

**Common failure.** Guessing at a timeline. "Probably next year" is a fabrication
with a friendly face, and the room will remember it.

## Exercise 6 — ISO against UK implementation guidance

**Task.** Explain the difference between ISO 19650 and the UK BIM Framework.
Forty-five seconds. Then state what it means for **a South African project**.

**Why this exercise.** The guidance is the most usable material available and the
most easily mistaken for the standard. The second half is the part presenters
skip, and it is the part that matters here.

**What good looks like.** ISO 19650 is the international standard. The UK BIM
Framework is **official implementation guidance for the UK** — useful, worth
reading, and not the standard's text. It is not a universal national annex, and
it is not automatically applicable in South Africa. Then the honest close: no
South African implementation guidance is registered for this programme, and
whether an applicable framework exists is an **open question**, not something to
fill in with UK material.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I named it as **guidance**, not as the standard.
- [ ] I said **UK** out loud.
- [ ] I said it is **not automatically applicable** here.
- [ ] I stated the South African position as **open**, not as absent-therefore-UK.
- [ ] I did not disparage the guidance — it is good material, correctly labelled.

**Common failure.** "It's basically the same thing, just explained better." That
is exactly the substitution the source hierarchy exists to prevent.

## Exercise 7 — Identify one Harrismith analogue and label it correctly

**Task.** Pick **one** Harrismith arrangement, explain what ISO-associated
principle it illustrates, and **say its mapping category out loud**. Sixty
seconds.

**Why this exercise.** Slide 13 is the module's honesty slide, and it fails
quietly — not by claiming compliance outright, but by describing an analogue in
language that sounds like equivalence.

**What good looks like.** Take the CDE state set — WIP, Shared, Published /
Authorised, Record / Retained. Explain that it illustrates controlled progression
and purposeful sharing. Then label it: **`LOCAL ANALOGUE OR INTERPRETATION`** — it
**resembles** ISO-associated practice, and conformity **has not been
demonstrated**. Note in passing that we say *Record / Retained*, not *Archived*.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I chose exactly one arrangement.
- [ ] I named the category **aloud**, in the four-category vocabulary.
- [ ] I used the word **resembles** or **illustrates** — never *complies* or
      *matches*.
- [ ] I used Harrismith's own term, unaltered.
- [ ] I did not claim the standard requires what we do.

**Common failure.** "This is our implementation of the ISO state model." Two
overclaims in eight words: that there is an ISO state model we have seen, and
that ours implements it.

## Exercise 8 — Identify one Harrismith gap or unverified matter

**Task.** Name one thing about Harrismith that **cannot** be mapped to ISO 19650
from available material, and explain what would be needed to resolve it. Forty-
five seconds.

**Why this exercise.** A presenter who only shows analogues has given a compliance
talk with cautious adjectives. The gaps are what make the mapping credible.

**What good looks like.** Any of these:

| Gap | What would resolve it |
|---|---|
| `Lead Delivery Party` against *lead appointed party* | A licensed copy of the standard, plus a recorded mapping decision |
| *appointed party* — **absent from our sources entirely** | The same |
| Whether our state model corresponds to the standard's | A licensed copy; a conformity assessment |
| Whether any South African implementation guidance applies | External research — not done |
| Whether we meet any requirement at all | **A clause-by-clause assessment that does not exist** |

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I named a **specific** gap, not "we've got gaps".
- [ ] I said what would be needed to resolve it.
- [ ] I did not resolve it by guessing.
- [ ] I did not present the gap as a failure — it is an unfinished mapping.
- [ ] I resisted substituting the ISO-associated term for ours "just to explain
      it".

**Common failure.** "Lead Delivery Party — that's basically the lead appointed
party." That is the exact substitution Module 2 recorded and deferred, made
casually, in an aside, where nobody writes it down.

## Exercise 9 — "Are we ISO-compliant?"

**Task.** Answer it. Thirty seconds. No notes.

**Why this exercise.** It is the question. It will be asked by whoever has the
most authority in the room, and it is usually asked hoping for a yes.

**What good looks like.**

> "No — and we've never claimed to be. Our BEP says it in writing: ISO 19650
> principles inform how we manage information, and that's a statement about
> influence. No compliance is claimed, and none has been assessed. There's no
> clause-by-clause assessment anywhere in this project, and there couldn't be —
> nobody here has worked through the standard against what we do. What we do have
> is a documented information-management approach that shows the principles
> working, which is a different and more useful thing for a training
> implementation."

**Success criteria**

- [ ] **No** was the first word, or near enough.
- [ ] I cited the **BEP's own statement**, not my own caution.
- [ ] I said no clause-by-clause assessment exists.
- [ ] I did not offer "we're probably close" or "we'd pass if assessed".
- [ ] I gave the positive close — influence and illustration — without
      retracting the no.
- [ ] I did not blame anyone for the absence. It is a deliberate position.

**Common failure.** "Not formally, no." *Formally* implies informal compliance
exists. It does not. There is no informal conformity, and the qualifier gives the
room the yes they wanted.

## Exercise 10 — Self-assessment: did I keep the three apart?

**Do this after every other exercise, and after every rehearsal of every slide.**

**Task.** Replay what you just said. For each substantive statement, name which
of three it was:

| | Class | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | **Principle** — published ISO scope | Would this survive someone reading `X1`/`X2`'s published scope? |
| **G** | **Guidance** — official implementation guidance | Did I say **UK** when I used it? |
| **E** | **Project evidence** — Harrismith | Did I attribute it to our documents rather than to the standard? |

Then the residue: anything that was **your own framing** — interpretation or
synthesis. **That is fine, provided you knew it at the time.**

**Success criteria**

- [ ] Every substantive statement got a letter — none was unclassifiable.
- [ ] No **E** statement was delivered in language that made it sound like **P**.
- [ ] Every **G** statement carried the word *UK*.
- [ ] No **P** statement went beyond published scope into requirement.
- [ ] My own framing was flagged **at the time**, not reconstructed afterwards.
- [ ] I said "I don't know" at least once where I genuinely did not.

**Common failure — and the one this whole module is built to prevent.** Delivering
a Harrismith statement with the confidence of a standard. It never sounds like a
lie. It sounds like expertise. That is exactly why it needs a checklist.

**If you cannot classify a sentence, do not say it.** Rewrite it until you can, or
drop it.

## Exercise 11 — The asset life cycle, without drawing a diagram

**Task.** Explain that information management runs across the asset life cycle —
**with no diagram, no whiteboard, no gestures at a ribbon**. Sixty seconds,
words only.

**Why this exercise.** The diagram is a crutch, and in this module it is a
dangerous one: the nearest thing to hand is a protected figure nobody here has
seen. If you can only teach this with a picture, you will eventually draw the
wrong picture.

**What good looks like.** You walk through decisions rather than phases: someone
decides the asset is needed; it gets designed, built, commissioned, handed over;
then someone operates and maintains it for decades; eventually someone alters or
replaces it. Each is a decision, each needs information. You flag the phase names
as your own words.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] No diagram, drawn or described as one.
- [ ] I described **decisions**, not stages.
- [ ] I said the labels were **my words, not official phase names**.
- [ ] I gave the operational span the weight it deserves.
- [ ] I used no numbering — no "stage 3", no "phase 2".

**Common failure.** Reciting a work-stage system from a previous job. It is
someone else's protected framework, it is jurisdiction-specific, and it is not
what the published scope says.

## Exercise 12 — Why information must outlive its authors

**Task.** Explain why information has to remain understandable after the team that
made it has gone. Forty-five seconds. Use one concrete example.

**Why this exercise.** It is Slide 4's take-away, and it is the point that
converts *life cycle* from an abstraction into something the audience feels.

**What good looks like.** The asset stands for decades; the delivery team disbands
in months. Whoever operates it will never meet the author. So a container that
only makes sense to the person who made it — a private naming habit, an
undocumented assumption, a model whose structure encodes someone's workflow —
fails the moment it matters. That is a management property, not a modelling skill.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I made the **time asymmetry** explicit — team months, asset decades.
- [ ] I gave one concrete example of information that fails without its author.
- [ ] I framed it as a **management** property.
- [ ] I did not claim the standard requires any specific measure.
- [ ] I did not invent an operational requirement for Harrismith.

**Common failure.** Sliding into "so we need good naming standards". True, and
Module 4's territory. The point here is *why*, not *what to configure*.

---

## Exercise 13 — Build the requirements-before-production sequence

**Task.** Say the six-step sequence from memory and explain **why each step
precedes the next**. Sixty seconds.

**Why this exercise.** Anyone can recite six nouns. The teaching is in the
*because*, and it is where a presenter is caught out.

**What good looks like.** Decision or purpose → information need → defined
requirement → planned production → checked delivery → use. And the reasoning: you
cannot know what is needed until you know what is being decided; you cannot write
a requirement for a need nobody has stated; you cannot plan production against an
undefined requirement; and delivering unchecked information means the recipient
does your checking, badly, later.

**Reference.** BEP §7.1, quotable: *"Production begins from a requirement, not
from availability of time or tools."*

**Success criteria**

- [ ] All six steps, in order.
- [ ] At least three **because** clauses, not just the nouns.
- [ ] I attributed the quotable line to **our BEP**.
- [ ] I said the sequence is **teaching structure**, not the standard's process.
- [ ] **No requirement acronym** passed my lips.

**Common failure.** Presenting the sequence as though it came from Part 2. It came
from our own BEP's production and delivery principles, arranged by the presenter.

## Exercise 14 — Find the decision behind a request

**Task.** Someone asks you for "the structural model, updated". Work backwards to
the **decision** the request actually serves — out loud, in questions. Forty-five
seconds.

**Why this exercise.** It is Slide 5 made operational, and it is the single most
useful habit in the module. Most information requests arrive as nouns; the useful
version is a decision.

**What good looks like.** You ask what they are deciding, by when, who else needs
to see it, how much they need to rely on it, and what form is usable to them. And
you notice that the answer changes the request: *"updated"* might mean coordinated
geometry, or a confirmed grid, or one dimension somebody could have got from a
drawing.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I asked what **decision** it supports before agreeing to anything.
- [ ] I asked at least four of the seven requirement questions.
- [ ] I showed how the answer **changed** the request.
- [ ] I did not treat "updated" as a specification.
- [ ] I stayed conversational — this is a colleague, not an audit.

**Common failure.** Turning it into an interrogation. Four questions asked
helpfully get answers; seven asked defensively get a complaint.

## Exercise 15 — "Why can't the modeller decide what's needed?"

**Task.** Answer it in thirty seconds, without insulting the modeller.

**Why this exercise.** It is asked defensively, usually by the most skilled person
in the room, and a clumsy answer costs their attention for the rest of the module.

**What good looks like.** They *do* decide a great deal — how to produce it well,
how to structure it, what is technically sound. What they cannot decide alone is
what someone else needs and why, because that lives outside their view of the
project. If nobody establishes it, the decision still gets made — by whoever
happens to be producing — which is exactly the accidental route Module 2
described.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I acknowledged what the modeller **does** decide, first.
- [ ] I located the missing knowledge outside their view, not in their ability.
- [ ] I connected it to the decision getting made by default.
- [ ] I did not say "that's above their pay grade" or anything like it.
- [ ] Thirty seconds.

**Common failure.** Answering as though the question were hostile. It usually
is not — it is someone who has been left to guess for years and would rather not.

## Exercise 16 — More information versus right information

**Task.** Explain the difference. Forty-five seconds. **Then say the sentence that
stops it becoming an argument for less.**

**Why this exercise.** Slide 6's content, plus the guard that most presenters
forget.

**What good looks like.** Right information supports an identified decision, has a
known originator, a defined recipient and purpose, arrives when needed, and can be
checked and maintained. More information is produced because the software can,
duplicated, delivered without purpose, too detailed to check. And then the guard:
**too little information creates decision risk, and that is the costlier failure.
This is not an argument for less — it is an argument for proportionate.**

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I gave at least three characteristics per side.
- [ ] I said the guard sentence, or my own version of it.
- [ ] I used the word **proportionate**, not *minimal* or *lean*.
- [ ] I tied "right" to a **stated purpose**, not to a quantity.
- [ ] I did not present the comparison as ISO's.

**Common failure.** Delivering it as a virtue list. The right-hand column is not
morally superior; it is fit for something specific.

## Exercise 17 — Excessive information creating risk

**Task.** Give **one** concrete example of too much information creating real
risk. Thirty seconds.

**Why this exercise.** The abstraction persuades nobody. One example does.

**What good looks like.** Any of: detail modelled beyond what anyone will maintain,
so it silently goes stale and someone relies on it; the same dimension described
in three containers that later disagree; a model so heavy the coordination review
gets skipped; specification-level detail in a concept model that reads as a
commitment. The risk is always the same shape — **unwarranted confidence**.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] One example, concrete enough to picture.
- [ ] I named the **risk**, not just the waste.
- [ ] I avoided naming a real project or person.
- [ ] I did not use a Harrismith example — none is recorded.
- [ ] Thirty seconds.

**Common failure.** Arguing cost rather than risk. Cost is an argument with the
budget holder; risk is an argument with everyone.

---

## Exercise 18 — Insufficient information creating risk

**Task.** Give **one** concrete example of too little information creating real
risk. Thirty seconds.

**Why this exercise.** It is the half that gets dropped when time runs short, and
dropping it converts Slide 6 into a minimalism argument.

**What good looks like.** A decision taken on information that never said how
reliable it was; a clash review run against a model missing a discipline, which
returns a clean result that means nothing; a handover that omits what an operator
needs, so it gets re-surveyed at cost. The risk shape here is **a decision made in
the dark that looks like a decision made in the light**.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] One example, concrete.
- [ ] I showed the decision being **wrongly trusted**, not merely delayed.
- [ ] I made it comparable in weight to Exercise 17's example.
- [ ] I did not attribute it to Harrismith.
- [ ] Thirty seconds.

**Common failure.** Choosing a trivially recoverable example. If the audience
hears *"and then we asked for it"*, the risk did not land.

## Exercise 19 — Harrismith's unestablished requirements, without a non-conformity claim

**Task.** Explain that Harrismith has no formal information requirements — and
that this is **not** evidence of non-conformity. Sixty seconds.

**Why this exercise.** The gap is real, the audience will notice it, and the
instinct is to apologise for it. Apologising concedes an assessment that never
happened.

**What good looks like.** The BEP records that no formal information requirements
were made available to this implementation, and — more importantly — that they are
**not invented**. What was developed for the training workflow is labelled
proposed governance or training assumption. If real requirements later arrive,
they take precedence over anything developed in their absence. And then the
separation: that is a **project state**, not an audit finding. Nobody has assessed
this project against the standard, so neither conformity nor non-conformity can be
claimed.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] I stated the gap plainly, without apology.
- [ ] I said the requirements were **not invented** — the discipline, not the
      deficiency.
- [ ] I separated **project state** from **audit finding**.
- [ ] I said no assessment exists **in either direction**.
- [ ] I did not offer "we'd probably be fine" or "we're technically
      non-compliant".

**Common failure.** "We're not compliant yet." Three errors in three words: it
concedes an assessment, implies a trajectory, and invents a destination nobody
has agreed.

## Exercise 20 — "What is level of information need?" — without inventing a definition

**Task.** Answer it in thirty seconds. **Give no formal definition.**

**Why this exercise.** It is the module's most reliable trap. The question is
asked innocently and answered badly by almost everyone, usually with a
half-remembered breakdown delivered with complete confidence.

**What good looks like.** In plain terms: how much detail and how much reliability
the information needs in order to serve its purpose — decided per purpose, not set
once for the project. Then the stop: there is a defined treatment in the standard,
you do not have it, and you are not going to reconstruct it from memory. Then the
project position: on Harrismith it is **not defined**, not established for any
container, **not inferred from what happens to be in the models**, and recorded as
an open gap.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] Plain-language concept only.
- [ ] **No** geometric / alphanumeric / documentation breakdown.
- [ ] **No** level, grade, scale or numbered tier.
- [ ] I said openly that I do not have the definition.
- [ ] I gave the Harrismith position, including *not inferred from model content*.
- [ ] I offered the licensed-copy route rather than an approximation.

**Common failure.** The confident three-part breakdown. It sounds like expertise,
it may even be roughly right, and it is exactly the fabrication this module exists
to prevent.

## Exercise 23 — An information-management function, without a job title

**Task.** Explain what an information-management function is, in forty-five
seconds, **without naming any job title** — no BIM Manager, no Coordinator, no
Task-Team Lead, no "the BIM person".

**Why this exercise.** The moment a title appears, the audience stops hearing
*responsibility* and starts hearing *headcount*. If you cannot describe the
function without the label, you have not separated them.

**What good looks like.** A function is something that must be performed for
information to be relied on — establishing what is needed, checking it,
authorising its release, deciding what it may be used for. Someone must hold it.
That someone may already exist, may hold several, and may be called anything at
all.

**Success criteria**

- [ ] No job title, in any form.
- [ ] I described **what must be performed**, not who performs it.

## Exercise 42 — Delivery, receipt and acceptance
