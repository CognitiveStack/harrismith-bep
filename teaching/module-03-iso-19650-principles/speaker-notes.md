## Slide 1 — ISO 19650 is an information-management framework · 1.5 min

### Purpose

Introduce ISO 19650 as a structured information-management framework — not a
software standard, not a modelling manual, not a naming convention — and set the
module's honesty position before anything else is said.

### Essential message

1. ISO 19650 is about **managing information**, not operating software.
2. It concerns the information needed for **decisions**: how it is specified,
   produced, managed, exchanged and recorded.
3. It applies **across built assets and organisations** — not to one asset type,
   one discipline or one company.
4. **We are not ISO-compliant, and this module does not claim we are.**
5. **I have not read the standard for this course.** What I can give you is its
   published scope, official guidance, and our own worked example.

### Suggested wording

> "Module 3 is about ISO 19650 — and I want to start by taking something away.
>
> When people hear 'the BIM standard', they picture software. A file-naming
> convention, maybe. Something you comply with by configuring a platform
> correctly.
>
> That's not what this is. ISO 19650 is an information-management framework. It's
> about the information a project needs to make decisions — how you say what you
> need, how it gets produced, how it's managed, how it moves between people, and
> how it's recorded so it can be relied on later. Buildings, infrastructure,
> organisations of any size.
>
> Here's the way I'd put it: ISO 19650 is about managing information so that the
> right people can rely on the right information for the right purpose. That's my
> sentence, not the standard's — I'll be careful about that distinction all the
> way through.
>
> And two things up front, because they change how you should listen to the next
> twenty minutes. **We are not ISO-compliant.** Our own BEP says that — in
> writing, deliberately. And **I haven't read the standard for this course.** It's
> a copyrighted document we don't hold. What I can give you is its published
> scope, some official guidance, and our own project as a worked example. Where
> the exact requirement matters, you need the standard itself."

### Shorter fallback

> "ISO 19650 is an information-management framework, not a software standard.
> It's about the information a project needs for decisions — how you specify it,
> produce it, manage it, exchange it and record it — across assets and across
> organisations. Two caveats before we start: we're not ISO-compliant and don't
> claim to be, and I haven't read the standard for this course. What I've got is
> its published scope, official guidance, and our own worked example."

|---|---|---|
| Part 1 concerns concepts and principles for information management using BIM | `X1` | **A1 — published, public metadata** |
| Its framework covers exchanging, recording, versioning and organising information | `X1` | **A1** |
| Its application extends across the asset life cycle | `X1` | **A1** |
| Applicable across asset types, organisations and procurement strategies | `X2` | **A1** |

**That is the entire external basis for this slide.** Four scope statements.
Everything else you say is Harrismith evidence or your own framing.

### Harrismith analogy

Not an analogy on this slide — an **admission**, and it is stronger for being the
project's own:

> BEP §11.2: ISO 19650 principles inform the information-management approach.
> *"This is a statement about influence. **No formal compliance with ISO 19650 is
> claimed**, and none has been established or assessed."*

Use it. It is more persuasive coming from the document than from you, and it
inoculates the whole module against the conformity misunderstanding.

If you need a second: BEP §11.3 records that **no ISO 19650 filename pattern is
imposed** on this project — which kills "it's a naming standard" in one line.

### Likely question

**"Is ISO 19650 a BIM software standard?"**

### Safe answer

> "No. Its published scope is information management — specifying, producing,
> managing, exchanging and recording information across an asset's life. Software
> is how you'd implement it. It isn't the subject. There's no product in it, no
> vendor in it, and no software that makes you compliant by being installed."

**Second likely question — "Do we need to buy the standard?"**

> "If you need the exact requirement, yes — someone does. This course teaches
> principles and how they play out on a real project. It can't tell you what any
> particular clause says, because I haven't read it and we don't hold it. For a
> real appointment with real obligations, you need a licensed copy. That's a
> genuine cost and I'd rather you heard it now."

**Do not** offer to summarise the standard's contents, describe its structure, or
say what "it says" about anything. You do not know.

## Slide 2 — It governs information, not a software platform · 1.5 min

### Purpose

Separate management principles from technology implementation. This is the
module's load-bearing correction: if it does not land, every later slide gets
heard as a platform feature.

### Essential message

1. A platform may **support** the process. It does not **be** the process.
2. **A CDE is not created by purchasing one.** Our BEP: *"it is not a folder
   tree."*
3. **Permissions do not create authority.** They support the process; they do not
   create professional or governance authority.
4. **A folder location does not establish information status.** State, version,
   revision, status and suitability are five separate things, and a new file
   version creates none of the others.
5. **Technology implements agreed arrangements** — decision first, configuration
   after.

### Suggested wording

> "Most of us already have the software. So let's be precise about what having it
> gets you.
>
> Our own BEP puts it well — and I want to flag that this is *our* wording, not
> the standard's. It says the Common Data Environment is an information-management
> process supported by technology. And then, bluntly: *it is not a folder tree.*
>
> That distinction does a lot of work. Buying a platform is procurement. A CDE is
> a process. The platform can host the process beautifully — or host nothing at
> all, which is the more common outcome.
>
> Two consequences. **Permissions aren't authority.** Being able to write to a
> folder tells you what the software will let you do. It tells you nothing about
> what you're permitted to decide. And **where a file sits doesn't tell you what
> it is.** Our BEP separates five things people routinely collapse — state,
> version, revision, status and suitability — and it makes the point that saving a
> new version creates none of the others. New version isn't new revision, isn't
> approval, isn't fit for a new purpose.
>
> There's a line in our own validation record I like even more: *folder names do
> not themselves prove ISO 19650 governance.* Which is us saying it about us.
>
> So: a platform can hold your information. It cannot decide who may rely on it,
> or for what. That's a human decision, written down somewhere — which is where
> Modules 1 and 2 come in. Module 1: the BEP is where the method gets written
> down. Module 2: someone has to actually hold each function. Module 3 — today —
> is the wider framework all of that sits inside."

### Shorter fallback

> "A platform supports the process; it isn't the process. Buying one doesn't give
> you a CDE. Permissions don't create authority — they tell you what the software
> allows, not what you're permitted to decide. And a folder location doesn't
> establish status: version, revision, state, status and suitability are five
> different things, and saving a new file creates only the first. Technology
> implements what the project agreed. It doesn't decide it."

### Authoritative external-source basis

**None.** This slide has **no A1 or A2 source at all**.

Every substantive statement is Harrismith wording used as a worked analogy —
`M3-S2-01`–`05`, all class `HARRISMITH`. That is a deliberate design decision,
and it constrains delivery: **you may not say the standard requires any of it.**
It may. You have not read it.

### Harrismith analogy

The whole slide is the analogy. Four BEP statements and one validation record:

| Point | Wording | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Process, not folder tree | *"an information-management process supported by technology… it is not a folder tree"* | BEP §6.1 |
| Permission ≠ authority | permissions *"support the process; they do not create professional or governance authority"* | BEP §6.9 |
| Five separate properties | a new platform version *"creates none of the others"* | BEP §6.8 |
| Decision precedes configuration | platform change follows a governance decision, not the reverse | BEP §12.1 |
| Folders prove nothing | *"Folder names do not themselves prove ISO 19650 governance"* | Increment-7C record |

**Attribute aloud at least once**: *"this is our BEP's wording, not the
standard's."* Say it once, early in the slide, and the whole sequence is safe.

### Likely question

**"Does using ACC make us ISO-compliant?"**

### Safe answer

> "No. A platform is a tool. Conformity — if you were pursuing it — would be about
> whether your information-management arrangements meet the standard's
> requirements, and it would need assessing against the standard itself. Nobody
> has done that here. Our own validation record makes the smaller version of the
> point: folder names don't prove governance. You can configure ACC perfectly and
> have no information management at all, and you can run good information
> management on tools you'd rather not have."

**Do not** add "but ACC helps a lot" as a softener. It converts a clean no into a
hedged yes, which is what the questioner wanted.

### Transition to Slide 3

> "Right — so it's a framework about information. But it isn't one document. There
> are two published parts that matter to us, and they answer genuinely different
> questions."

### Evidence classification and warning

**`HARRISMITH`** — all five substantive statements (`M3-S2-01`–`05`).
**`INTERP`** — technology implements agreed arrangements; the Modules 1/2/3
connection (`M3-S2-06`, `M3-S2-07`).
**`SYNTH`** — *"a platform can hold your information…"*; the bought-a-platform
trap (`M3-S2-08`, `M3-S2-09`).
**`EXCLUDED`** — that any of the five is an ISO definition or requirement
(`M3-S2-10`).

**Delivery warning — this is the module's highest overclaim risk.** Five crisp,

**Second warning: the Module 1 and 2 callback is three lines.** Roughly fifteen
seconds. It is orientation, not revision. If you find yourself re-explaining what
a BEP is, you have lost a third of the slide and Slide 3 pays for it.

## Slide 3 — Part 1 and Part 2 answer different questions · 1.0 min

### Purpose

Distinguish the two currently published core parts, and record — without teaching
— that both are under revision.

### Essential message

1. **`ISO 19650-1:2018`** — concepts and principles; the framework; exchanging,
   recording, versioning and organising information; **the whole asset life
   cycle**.
2. **`ISO 19650-2:2018`** — an information-management process for the **delivery
   phase**, including the exchanges within it; applicable across asset types,
   organisations and procurement strategies.
3. **Part 1 explains the framework; Part 2 applies a process to delivery.**
4. **Both are under revision** — and **the 2018 editions are what is current.**

### Suggested wording

> "Two published parts matter to us today, and they answer different questions.
>
> Part 1 — the 2018 edition — answers *what is this about, and on what
> principles?* Concepts and principles. The framework itself. Exchanging,
> recording, versioning and organising information. And its reach is the whole
> asset life cycle — not just the bit where we're designing and building.
>
> Part 2, also 2018, answers a narrower and more practical question: *how is
> information managed while an asset is being delivered?* It's an
> information-management process for the delivery phase, including the exchanges
> that happen within it. And it's meant to work across different asset types,
> different organisations and different procurement routes.
>
> So: Part 1 explains the conceptual framework. Part 2 applies a process to asset
> delivery.
>
> One thing you should hear from me rather than find out later. **Both of those
> published editions are currently under revision.** That's worth knowing. It is
> not worth teaching — a draft isn't a requirement, and I'm not going to tell you
> what's in one, because I don't know. Today, and until a replacement actually
> publishes, the 2018 editions are the current published editions and they're what
> we're working from."

> asset life cycle. Part 2, 2018 — an information-management process for the
> delivery phase and the exchanges inside it, applicable across asset types,

> applies a process to delivery. Both are under revision — but a draft isn't a

## Slide 4 — Information management runs across the asset life cycle · 1.5 min

### Purpose

Show that managed information supports decisions across the life of an asset, not
only while a model is being produced — and kill the single-eternal-model
misreading before it takes hold.

### Essential message

1. Part 1's stated application **extends across the asset life cycle**.
2. Decisions needing information run from **strategic need** through **design,
   construction, handover, operation** and eventually **alteration or end of
   life**.
3. **The asset outlives the team.** Information must stay understandable to
   people who were never in the room.
4. **What continues is managed information, not one unchanged file.**
5. Information produced with no identified future purpose has little lasting
   value.

### Suggested wording

> "Here's something that surprises people about the published scope of Part 1:
> its application runs across the whole life cycle of the asset. Not the project.
> The asset.
>
> Think about what that actually covers. Somebody decides the asset is needed at
> all. Then it's designed, then it's built, then it's commissioned and handed
> over. Then — and this is the long bit — somebody operates it and maintains it,
> for decades. And eventually somebody alters it, or renews it, or takes it down.
> Every one of those is a decision, and every one of them needs information.
>
> I want to be careful: those are my words for the phases, not official phase
> names. I'm describing where decisions happen, not reciting a stage model.
>
> Now the consequence, and it's the one worth taking away. **The asset outlives
> the team.** This building might stand for fifty years. Nobody in this room will
> be available to explain what we meant. So the information has to make sense
> without us — it has to remain understandable and usable a long way outside the
> context it was authored in. That's a management property. It's not something
> you get by modelling harder.
>
> And one thing this does *not* mean. It doesn't mean one model file sails
> serenely through fifty years. Information gets superseded, replaced, re-authored,
> converted, thrown away. What continues isn't a file. It's the ability to know
> what information you've got, whether you can trust it, and what it's good for."

### Shorter fallback

> "Part 1's stated reach is the whole asset life cycle — need, design,
> construction, handover, operation, and eventually alteration or end of life.
> Each of those is a decision that needs information. Which matters because the
> asset outlives the team: the information has to make sense to people who were
> never involved. And that's continuity of *managed information*, not one model
> file lasting fifty years."

### Authoritative public-source basis

| Statement | Source | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Part 1's application extends across the asset life cycle | `X1` | **A1 — published, public metadata** |

**One statement. That is the entire external basis for this slide.** The six

## Slide 5 — Information requirements come before information production · 1.5 min

### Purpose

Show that nobody can decide what to produce until someone establishes what is
needed, why, and when it supports a decision. Section B's hinge.

### Essential message

1. The sequence is **decision or purpose → information need → defined requirement
   → planned production → checked delivery → use**.
2. **"Production begins from a requirement, not from availability of time or
   tools"** (BEP §7.1).
3. A useful requirement answers roughly seven questions — what decision, who
   needs it, what, when, how reliable, how checked, in what form.
4. **UK guidance** adds that requirements should be established **appointment by
   appointment**.
5. Harrismith has **no formal information requirements available — and does not
   invent them**.

### Suggested wording

> "So how does a team know what to produce?
>
> On a lot of projects the honest answer is: it doesn't. Production starts because
> people are available and the software is open, and the purpose gets attached
> afterwards — usually at the point somebody asks what a drawing is for.
>
> Our BEP puts the alternative in one line: *production begins from a requirement,
> not from availability of time or tools.* And elsewhere: information production
> is driven by defined requirements, not by assumption about what might be wanted.
>
> The sequence runs one way. A decision or a purpose. Which creates an information
> need. Which gets written down as a defined requirement. Then production is
> planned. Then it's checked and delivered. Then it's used. Reverse any two of
> those and you get information that exists but nobody can rely on.
>
> What's in a requirement? Roughly seven questions. What decision does this
> support? Who needs it? What information, exactly? When? How reliable does it
> have to be? How will it be checked? And in what form? Our BEP lists most of
> those — container, originator, recipient, purpose, format, the exchange it
> serves, the checking and authorisation requirements.
>
> There's a UK point worth adding here, and I'm flagging it as UK. The UK BIM
> Framework — which is official implementation guidance, not the standard — makes
> the point that these requirements get established appointment by appointment.
> Not settled once at the start and assumed to hold for everyone.
>
> And then our own position, which is the uncomfortable one. **We have no formal
> information requirements.** None were made available to this implementation. The
> BEP says so, and it says something more important: they are not invented. Where
> we needed something to work with, it's labelled a training assumption or
> proposed governance — never presented as a client requirement. Which is why our
> delivery schedule has event-triggered entries and TBDs where a normal project
> would have dates."

### Shorter fallback

> "Production should start from a requirement, not from having time and a licence.
> Decision, then need, then defined requirement, then planned production, then
> checked delivery, then use. A requirement says what decision it serves, who
> needs it, what, when, how reliable, how it's checked and in what form. UK
> guidance — flagged as UK — adds that this happens appointment by appointment.
> And on this project we have no formal requirements available, so we labelled
> what we developed as training assumptions rather than inventing client
> requirements."

| Part 1's framework covers **specifying**, exchanging, recording, versioning and organising information | `X1` | A1 — published |

## Slide 6 — The right information, not simply more information · 1.5 min

### Purpose

Correct the belief that better BIM means more information — and close Section B by
turning requirements-first into a judgement about proportion.

### Essential message

1. Information is proportionate to **the decision, the recipient, the delivery
   event, the asset or element, the required reliability and the context of
   use**.
2. **Completeness is purpose-dependent.** *Complete for what* is the only
   answerable version of the question.
3. More detail carries a real burden — coordination, checking, revision,
   maintenance.
4. **Too little is also a failure.** Insufficient information creates decision
   risk. This is not an argument for minimalism.
5. **Harrismith has not established level of information need**, and records it
   as a gap.

### Suggested wording

> "There's a belief that better BIM means more information. More elements, more
> parameters, more detail, higher fidelity. And it's wrong in a way that costs
> real money.
>
> Useful information is proportionate. Proportionate to the decision being made,
> to whoever has to act on it, to the exchange it's part of, to the thing it
> describes, to how much weight the decision puts on it, and to what's even
> knowable at that point. A door handle and a structural frame do not warrant the
> same treatment.
>
> So compare two piles. On one side: information produced because the software
> could produce it. Duplicated across containers. Delivered with no defined
> purpose. Detail nobody can check or maintain. No clear recipient. On the other:
> it supports an identified decision, it has a known originator, a defined
> recipient and purpose, it arrives when it's needed, and it can be checked,
> maintained and relied on for that use. The second pile is usually smaller. But
> that's a consequence, not the goal.
>
> Because — and please hear this half too — **too little information is also a
> failure.** It creates decision risk, and it's the more expensive failure of the
> two. I'm not arguing for less. I'm arguing for proportionate.
>
> There's a concept here usually called level of information need — how much
> detail and reliability is actually needed for a given purpose. I'm going to
> describe it in plain words and stop there, because I don't have a definition I
> can stand behind. And on our project, we've done the same thing, in writing:
> level of information need is **not defined**. Not established for any container.
> And — this is the sharp bit — **not inferred from what happens to be in the
> models**. Observed content is not a requirement. It's recorded as an information
> gap and it stays an open decision."

### Shorter fallback

> "More information isn't better information. What's useful is proportionate — to
> the decision, the recipient, the exchange, the element, the reliability needed
> and the context. Complete is only answerable as *complete for what*. And the
> failure runs both ways: too much creates cost, ambiguity and maintenance
> nobody's resourced; too little creates decision risk. On this project, level of
> information need is not defined and isn't inferred from what's in the models —
> it's recorded as an open gap."

### Harrismith analogy

**An unresolved position, quotable almost verbatim** — Model / Information

### Likely question

**"Does more detail mean a better BIM deliverable?"**

## Slide 7 — Information-management functions are not automatically job titles · 1.5 min

### Purpose

Show that information-management activities must be **fulfilled**, and that
fulfilling them is not the same as inventing a job title for each one. Separate
five things the audience collapses into one.

### Essential message

1. **UK guidance** explains information management in terms of **functions**
   rather than requiring every function to become a new job title.
2. The **appointing party** must ensure the information-management function is
   fulfilled; the **lead appointed party** must ensure the relevant function is
   fulfilled. *(Guidance, UK.)*
3. **One person may perform several functions** — and **combining them does not
   merge their authorities**.
4. **A title does not prove the function was assigned.**
5. Activity, function, appointment, job title and participant are **five
   different things**.

### Suggested wording

> "Section C is about who actually does this. And I want to start by defusing an
> argument that happens on every project: whether you need to hire a BIM Manager.
>
> Hold five things apart. There's the **activity** — something that has to happen
> for information to be trustworthy. There's the **function** — the defined
> responsibility for making it happen. There's the **appointment** — who's
> engaged and on what terms. There's the **job title** — what someone's employer
> calls them. And there's the **person** doing it on the day.
>
> Now, the UK BIM Framework — and I'm flagging that as UK implementation guidance,
> not the standard's text — talks about information management in terms of
> functions rather than requiring every function to become a new job title. It
> says the appointing party has to ensure the information-management function is
> fulfilled, and the lead appointed party has to ensure the relevant function is
> fulfilled. **Ensure it's fulfilled.** Not *create a post*.
>
> I want to be careful about how far that goes. I am **not** telling you the
> standard says no BIM Manager is required. I haven't read it, and that's a much
> bigger claim than my sources support. What I can say is that the guidance frames
> this as functions.
>
> Our own project does the same thing. It defines BIM Manager, BIM Coordinator,
> Task-Team Lead, Author, Checker, CDE Administration — and it names nobody at
> all. Every holder is TBD. And it's blunt about combination: combining roles does
> not combine the functions. There's a lovely example — approving a governance
> change as BIM Manager is a different act from applying it as CDE Administrator,
> even when it's one person doing both within a minute of each other.
>
> So the question worth asking isn't *do we have a BIM Manager*. It's: is the
> function assigned, does the person know they hold it, and is it actually being
> performed?"

### Shorter fallback

> "Five different things: the activity, the function, the appointment, the job
> title, the person. UK guidance — flagged as UK — frames information management
> as functions that must be **ensured fulfilled**, rather than requiring a new job
> title for each. One person can hold several, and combining them doesn't merge
> their authorities. Our project defines the functions and names nobody. The
> question isn't whether a title exists — it's whether the function is assigned,
> understood and performed."

### Authoritative guidance basis

| Statement | Source | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Information management framed as **functions rather than new job titles** | **`X6`** | **A2 — implementation guidance** |
| The **appointing party** must ensure the information-management function is fulfilled | **`X6`** | **A2** |
| The **lead appointed party** must also ensure the relevant function is fulfilled | **`X6`** | **A2** |

**There is no A1 statement on this slide at all.** Everything external is UK
guidance; everything concrete is Harrismith.

### Jurisdiction note

**`OFFICIAL IMPLEMENTATION GUIDANCE — UK CONTEXT`.** All three statements above.
Labelled on the slide and said aloud.

**The forbidden extension:** *"ISO 19650 says no BIM Manager is required."* The
guidance describes functions. It does not tell us what the standard requires
about titles, and the standard has not been read. Prohibition 21.

### Harrismith analogy

Functions defined, **holders empty** — BEP §4.6, §5.7, §5.8, §5.9. Three sourced
lines:

- **"Combining roles does not combine the functions."** A participant performing
  two must know *"which function they are performing at each decision point"*
  (§5.11).
- Author and Checker may combine where independence is not available — but
  *"self-checking is still a checking act with a defined requirement, not an
  omission of one"*, and **"independence is never claimed where it does not
  exist"** (§5.8).
- **"Platform access is not delegation"** (§5.11).

Category: **`LOCAL ANALOGUE OR INTERPRETATION`.** It illustrates the principle. It
is **not** evidence that the Harrismith model is an ISO implementation.

### Likely question

**"Does ISO require a BIM Manager?"**

### Safe answer

> "I can't tell you what the standard requires about titles — I haven't read it,
> and I'm not going to guess. What I can tell you is how the UK guidance frames
> it, and I'll flag that it's UK guidance rather than the standard: it describes
> information management in terms of functions, and puts the obligation on the
> appointing party and the lead appointed party to **ensure the function is
> fulfilled**. That's a different thing from requiring a post. So the practical
> question is whether the work is assigned and being done — by someone, under some
> title, on some contract. If you want the definitive answer on titles, that's a
> licensed-copy conversation."

**Second likely question — "Can one person perform several information-management
functions?"**

> "Yes, and on a project this size they usually do. The thing that matters is that
> combining the roles doesn't combine the authorities. Our BEP's example is the
> clearest one I know: approving a governance change as BIM Manager and applying
> it as CDE Administrator are two different acts, even when it's one person within
> a minute. They have to know which one they're exercising. And where combining
> reduces independence — an author checking their own work, say — the limitation
> gets recorded rather than glossed over. Independence is never claimed where it
> doesn't exist."

### Transition to Slide 8

> "So — functions get assigned to somebody. Which raises the question of what we
> call those somebodies. And here we hit a genuine problem, because there are two
> vocabularies in this room and they are not the same vocabulary."

### Evidence classification

**`GUIDANCE` — UK** — functions rather than job titles; both ensure-fulfilment

**`HARRISMITH` — analogue** — one participant may hold several; combining does

**`SYNTH`** — the required message (`M3-S7-12`).
**`EXCLUDED`** — *"ISO says no BIM Manager is required"*; title equals function;

### Delivery warning

**The over-extension.** This slide's guidance is genuinely liberating — it lets a
small team stop arguing about org charts — and that makes it tempting to push it

**The opposite failure.** Do not let it become an argument that titles are

**Name nobody.** No holder, no organisation, no appointment exists in the sources.
An invented example — *"say our BIM Manager is the architect"* — creates an
appointment out of a teaching aid, and it will be repeated.

### Essential message

1. A container is a **managed unit of information** for which responsibility,
   status, revision and permitted use can be controlled. *(Working description,
   not a definition.)*
2. Models, drawings, schedules, specifications, reports, data files — **examples,
   not a closed list**.
3. **Originator responsibility stays with the producing task team.** *"No
   downstream act relieves it."*
4. **Federation does not merge authorship or responsibility.**
5. **Folder location does not establish authorship or status.**
6. **Sharing makes information available for a purpose; it transfers nothing.**

### Likely question

**"Is a Revit model an information container?"**

### Safe answer

> "It can be — it's the most familiar example. But two cautions. A container isn't
> necessarily one file; different implementations draw the boundary differently,
> and I can't tell you where the standard draws it. And a model is *one* container
> among many — the drawing, the schedule, the report and the data file are
> containers too, and on a lot of projects they carry the decisions that actually
> get made. Treating the model as the container is how everything else ends up
> ungoverned."

**Second likely question — "Is the federated model one information container?"**

> "I'd resist that framing. Our BEP is direct about it: federation doesn't merge
> authorship or responsibility. Aggregating models creates a coordination
> artefact, not a jointly-authored model — each contributed container keeps its
> own originator, state and technical responsibility. Federation is a lens for
> looking at several containers at once. Nobody becomes responsible for another
> team's content by appearing alongside it."

**Third likely question — "Does sharing a model transfer responsibility?"**

> "No. Consumption doesn't transfer the originator's technical responsibility —
> they remain answerable for what they produced. But the receiving team picks up a
> responsibility of its own: whether the information was appropriate to the use,
> whether it was current, and whether the use was within the stated purpose. Both
> exist at the same time, and neither cancels the other. That's the bit people get
> wrong in both directions — either 'I shared it, it's your problem now', or 'I
> just used what you gave me'."

**Fourth likely question — "Does a file's folder determine its status?"**

> "No. Our BEP has the line I'd use: where a container sits tells you where it
> sits. The originator is recorded, not deduced — and the same goes for status.
> State, version, revision, status and suitability are separate properties, and
> moving a file doesn't change any of them. How states actually work is Module 4;
> today the point is just that location isn't evidence."
