# Module 1 — Speaker Notes, Slides 1–3

**Status:** Working notes. **Not a script, and not final.**

These notes exist to support speaking in your own words. The *essential message*
is what must be communicated. The *suggested wording* is one way of getting
there — it is a starting point to react against, not a line to memorise. If the
suggested wording feels unlike you, the notes are working correctly: replace it.

Read the essential message before delivery. Do not read the suggested wording
during delivery.

---

## Slide 1 — What Is a BIM Execution Plan?

*Subtitle: The Harrismith Fire Station as a worked example*

### Essential message

Four things, in this order:

1. A BEP is an **operating agreement** about how a project team handles
   information — not, first and foremost, a document.
2. It concerns **multidisciplinary** information: the point is the interfaces
   between teams, not any one team's internal work.
3. **Harrismith Fire Station** is the worked example for everything that
   follows, and it is a training and reference implementation.
4. **Triviron** is where this is going — the reason for learning it on
   Harrismith rather than in the abstract.

Do not define the BEP here. The definition lands on Slide 3, after the problem.

### Suggested wording

> "This is about a BIM Execution Plan — but before the name does any damage, let
> me say what it actually is. It's an agreement between the people on a project
> about how they will handle information. Not one team's information. Everyone's,
> and specifically the places where it has to meet.
>
> I'm going to use a real worked example throughout: the Harrismith Fire Station.
> I should be straight about what that is — it's a training and reference
> implementation. There's no appointment behind it. What it does have is a
> complete, properly governed documentation set, developed the way a real one
> would be, which makes it a good thing to learn on.
>
> And the reason I've been working on it is Triviron. We'll need a BEP for a
> multidisciplinary Triviron project, and I'd rather make my mistakes here."

### Shorter fallback

> "A BIM Execution Plan is a project team's agreement about how they'll handle
> information together. I'll use the Harrismith Fire Station — a training
> project — as the worked example, and at the end I'll come back to what it means
> for Triviron."

### Likely questions

| Question | Direction of answer |
|---|---|
| "Is this a real project?" | Answer plainly: it is a training and reference implementation, structured as a real project BEP would be, with no appointment behind it. Do not oversell it — the honesty is what makes the rest credible. |
| "Do we need one of these?" | Do not answer yet. "Hold that — slide 2 is exactly that question." Answering here spends the problem before you have set it up. |
| "Is this an ISO 19650 thing?" | ISO 19650 principles inform the approach. No compliance or certification is claimed for Harrismith. If pressed, say that plainly and offer to come back to it — Module 3 territory. |

### Transition to Slide 2

Move from *what it is* to *why anyone bothered*. Something like: "So why does a
project need one written down? Here's what happens when it isn't."

## Slide 2 — Without an agreed execution plan, coordination becomes accidental

### Essential message

Two things:

1. Seven specific questions arise on every multidisciplinary project, and if
   they are not answered deliberately, they get answered accidentally —
   inconsistently, late, and differently by different people.
2. **Good individual models do not automatically create good project
   information.** The problem is not competence. It is the interfaces.

The audience should recognise this slide from their own experience. If it lands
as an abstraction, it has failed.

### Suggested wording

> "On any project with more than one discipline, the same questions come up.
>
> Who produces each piece of information. Which version is the current one. When
> it has to arrive. Where it has to be put. Who checks it. Who is allowed to say
> it can be relied on. And what happens when two disciplines disagree.
>
> Those questions get answered on every project. The only question is whether
> they get answered deliberately, up front, by the team — or accidentally,
> halfway through, by whoever happens to be in the room.
>
> Here's the part I want to be careful about. This is not a slide about people
> doing bad work. Every model on a project like this can be excellent, produced
> by someone who knows exactly what they're doing — and the project information
> can still be a mess. Good individual models do not automatically create good
> project information. The failure is never in the middle of somebody's model.
> It's at the edges, between them."

### Shorter fallback

> "Seven questions come up on every multidisciplinary project — who produces it,
> which version is current, when, where, who checks it, who authorises it, and
> how disagreements get resolved. They get answered either way. A BEP is the
> team answering them on purpose. And note: good individual models don't
> automatically add up to good project information."

### Likely questions

| Question | Direction of answer |
|---|---|
| "Isn't this just project management?" | Overlaps, genuinely. The distinction is that a BEP governs *information* specifically — its state, its purpose, its authority. Concede the overlap rather than defending a boundary. |
| "We manage fine without one." | Do not argue. Ask what happens on their project when two versions of the same drawing exist. Let the answer make the point. |
| "This sounds like blame." | Take it seriously and answer directly — that is what the teaching statement is for. The claim is the opposite of blame: the individual work is assumed to be good. |
| "Who decides the answers?" | That is the next section. Say so and move. |

### Transition to Slide 3

The problem is now on the table. Offer the definition as the answer to it: "So —
that's what a BEP is for. Here's what it actually is."

## Slide 3 — A BEP is the agreed operating plan for project information

### Essential message

Three things:

1. The definition: **a BEP defines how the project team will produce, manage,
   exchange, coordinate, review and deliver information to satisfy the project's
   information requirements.** Six verbs. They are the spine of the rest of the
   talk.
2. What a BEP is not — five common substitutes, each of which is a real thing
   that is not a BEP.
3. Most importantly: **a BEP is not proof that the agreed process is actually
   being implemented.** Agreement and implementation are different states.

If only one thing survives from this slide, it should be the six verbs. If two
things survive, the second is that last "is not".

### Suggested wording

> "So here's the definition. A BIM Execution Plan defines how the project team
> will produce, manage, exchange, coordinate, review and deliver information —
> to meet what the project actually needs.
>
> Six verbs. Produce, manage, exchange, coordinate, review, deliver. Everything
> else in this presentation is one of those six opened up.
>
> Now let me spend a moment on what it isn't, because these are the five things
> people usually have in mind instead.
>
> It's not a Revit manual. Modelling conventions are in there, but that's a part,
> not the whole. It's not a naming convention — naming is the visible bit, so it
> ends up standing in for everything invisible around it. It's not a company
> template dropped into a project unchanged; a template hasn't agreed anything
> with *this* team. And it's not the BIM manager's private procedure — if one
> person knows it, there's no agreement, there's just a preference.
>
> And the last one, which is the one I'd ask you to remember. A BEP is not proof
> that any of it is actually happening. It's what the team agreed to do.
> Whether they're doing it is a separate question, with separate evidence. I'll
> come back to that, because it's the most honest thing I can tell you about the
> example I'm using."

### Shorter fallback

> "A BEP defines how the team will produce, manage, exchange, coordinate, review
> and deliver information. Six verbs — that's the whole structure. What it isn't:
> a Revit manual, a naming convention, a company template copied across, or one
> person's private procedure. And it isn't evidence that any of it is actually
> being done — that's a separate question."

### Likely questions

| Question | Direction of answer |
|---|---|
| "What are 'information requirements'?" | What the project needs the information for, and who needs it. Keep it that simple here. Note honestly that on Harrismith no formal client information requirements have been established — they are recorded as a gap rather than invented. |
| "So it's a big document?" | It is written down, yes — but the document is the record of the agreement, not the agreement. The agreement is what the team will actually do. |
| "Who writes it?" | Next section. |
| "How is that different from a BIM standard?" | A standard governs a technical subject — naming, coordinates, templates. A BEP governs the process, and standards sit underneath it. |
| "If it's not proof of implementation, what is?" | Evidence of the acts themselves: the checks, the authorisations, the transitions, the delivery records. Flag it as coming later in the talk. |

### Transition to next section

The definition is set. Move to the people: "Six verbs — and none of them happen
by themselves. So who does them, and who is allowed to say yes?"

## Notes on using these notes

- **Slide 2 is the one to rehearse.** It is where the audience either recognises
  the problem or does not, and everything after it depends on that.
- **The three honesty statements are not optional** — training implementation,
  approved with conditions and not published, agreement is not implementation.
  Slide 1 carries the first; Slide 3 seeds the third. See
  [`README.md`](README.md) section 7.
- **Fallbacks exist for time pressure, not for difficulty.** If the session is
  running long, use them; do not use them to avoid a question you would rather
  not take.
- Speaker notes for Slides 4–14 do not exist yet.
