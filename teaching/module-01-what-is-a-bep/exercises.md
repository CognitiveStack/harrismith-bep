# Module 1 — Exercises

**Status:** Initial exercise set. Teaching material; **not governance.**

These are rehearsal exercises for the presenter, not assessments for an
audience. They are designed to be done aloud, alone, before the presentation is
delivered to anyone.

**How to use them.** Do them out loud. Time them. Do not write out an answer and
read it — the point is to be able to speak without notes, and writing first
defeats that. Exercise 5 is done after each of the others.

---

## Exercise 1 — Explain the need, without the phrase

**Task.** Explain for **30 seconds** why a project needs a BEP, **without using
the phrase "BIM Execution Plan"** — and without "BEP", "BIM", "ISO", "CDE",
"information container" or "workflow".

**Why this exercise.** The phrase does the explaining for you, badly. Remove it
and you are forced to describe the actual problem. If you cannot do this one, the
presentation will be a vocabulary lesson.

**What good looks like:** you describe a situation, not a document. Multiple
teams, information that has to fit together, and a set of questions nobody has
answered on purpose.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I describe a problem rather than a deliverable?
- [ ] Would someone with no construction background follow it?
- [ ] Did I stay within 30 seconds?
- [ ] Did any banned term slip through?

**Common failure:** substituting a synonym — "digital delivery framework" — and
carrying on. That is the same sentence with a different costume.

---

## Exercise 2 — Two minutes, to a project owner who does not use Revit

**Task.** Explain the BEP in **two minutes** to a project owner. They are
intelligent, commercially focused, and have never opened a model. They are
deciding whether this is worth paying for.

**Why this exercise.** This is the real audience for most BEP conversations, and
the one most likely to be lost by a technical explanation.

**What good looks like:** you talk about risk, rework, decisions and
accountability. You mention software once at most, and only as the place where
something happens.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I name a consequence they care about — cost, delay, rework, a decision made on stale information?
- [ ] Did I explain who becomes accountable for what?
- [ ] Did I avoid explaining how any software works?
- [ ] Did I say what they would have to decide or approve?

**Common failure:** describing the *contents* of a BEP — thirteen sections, six
supporting resources — instead of what it does for them. The structure is the
answer to a question they have not asked.

---

## Exercise 3 — Five competent models, one failed project

**Task.** Explain why **five technically competent discipline models** may still
fail as coordinated project information.

**Why this exercise.** This is the intellectual core of the module. It is the
argument behind the Slide 2 teaching statement, and it is the point most likely
to be challenged by someone who is good at their job.

**What good looks like:** you locate the failure at the interfaces, not in the
models. Concretely: no agreement on which version is current; no agreement on
when each is shared or for what purpose; no agreement on who checks the fit
between them; no route for resolving a disagreement; no one authorised to say a
model may now be relied on.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I concede that the individual work is good — explicitly?
- [ ] Did I name at least three distinct interface failures?
- [ ] Did I avoid implying anyone is incompetent?
- [ ] Could I say this to the five people who made those models?

**Common failure:** drifting into a clash-detection answer. Clash detection is
one technique within coordination, not the whole of it — and geometric clashes
are not the only way information fails to fit.

---

## Exercise 4 — What Triviron would have to decide for itself

**Task.** Identify which parts of the Harrismith approach would need
**project-specific decisions** before use on Triviron — and which parts transfer
as they stand.

**Why this exercise.** It is the difference between reusing a method and copying
a document. It is also the honest limit of what Harrismith can give Triviron.

**What good looks like:** you can name at least six decisions Triviron must take
for itself, and explain for each why Harrismith's position cannot simply be
inherited.

**Reference.** The transfer table in
[`source-map.md`](source-map.md) subject 10 lists seven such questions, each
derived from something Harrismith records as unestablished or unresolved. Do the
exercise first, then check against it.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I separate *structure and method* from *project-specific positions*?
- [ ] Did I avoid asserting any Triviron fact? (No Triviron information exists in the repository.)
- [ ] Did I name the roles and authorities that Triviron must establish?
- [ ] Did I notice that Harrismith's own unresolved items — appointing party, information requirements, CDE mapping, standards — are precisely the transfer questions?

**Common failure:** concluding "we copy Harrismith and change the names". That is
the template error the module exists to prevent.

---

## Exercise 5 — Self-assessment: software, documents or managed information?

**Task.** After each of the exercises above, review what you actually said.
Which of the three was it about?

| If the explanation centred on | It was really about | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| Revit, Navisworks, ACC, Forma, files, folders, exports | **Software** | You explained tools. The audience learned what you use, not what you manage |
| Sections, tables, matrices, schedules, the document itself | **Documents** | You explained an artefact. The audience learned that paperwork exists |
| Who produces what, for whom, by when, checked by whom, relied on for what | **Managed information** | This is BIM Management. This is the target |

**Do this honestly.** The software answer is the most seductive because it is the
easiest to be specific about. The document answer is the most common among people
who have just finished reading a BEP.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Where did most of my sentences sit?
- [ ] Did I mention a software product before I mentioned a person or a decision?
- [ ] If I removed every product name, would the explanation survive?
- [ ] If I removed every document name, would the explanation survive?

The last two are the real test. A good explanation of BIM Management survives
both deletions.

# Exercises for Slides 4–8

Exercises 1–5 above rehearse the opening argument. The eight below rehearse the
nine-minute middle — roles, template versus project BEP, the document set, the
two matrices, and delivery planning.

Same method: aloud, timed, without writing an answer first.

---

## Exercise 6 — Why the BIM manager is not the sole owner

**Task.** In **one minute**, explain why the BIM manager should not be described
as the sole owner of the BEP.

**Why this exercise.** It is the single most common misconception about the
document, and it makes every other governance conversation harder — because if
one person owns the BEP, everyone else is entitled to ignore it.

**What good looks like:** you separate at least three functions — drafting,
agreement, approval, implementation — and you make clear that distributed does
**not** mean vague.

**Reference.** BEP §5.5 lists what the BIM Manager is *not* automatically:
Appointing Party, Lead Delivery Party, design approver, discipline technical
lead, contractual decision-maker. The IM matrix note on governance-change
authority states that **no single universal approver exists** and that unlimited
authority is not assigned to the BIM Manager.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I name at least three distinct functions?
- [ ] Did I say who is *consulted* as well as who drafts?
- [ ] Did I avoid making it sound like nobody is accountable?
- [ ] Did I avoid naming a person? (Every holder is TBD.)
- [ ] One minute or less?

**Common failure:** replacing one owner with a committee. "Everyone owns it" is
as wrong as "the BIM manager owns it", and less useful.

---

## Exercise 7 — Template versus project-specific, without software

**Task.** Compare a BEP template with a project-specific BEP **without referring
to any software** — no Revit, ACC, Forma, Navisworks, no file formats, no
folders.

**Why this exercise.** The distinction is about decisions, not tools. Removing
software makes it impossible to hide behind a description of a platform.

**What good looks like:** the template *asks*; the project BEP *answers*. You
give at least two concrete examples of a question and the kind of answer a real
project would have to supply.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I get through it with no product name?
- [ ] Did I give a concrete example of an answered decision?
- [ ] Did I make the point that filled-in headings are not answers?
- [ ] Did I label the distinction as my framing rather than attributing it to the Harrismith documents?

**Common failure:** describing the template as "generic" and the project BEP as
"detailed". Detail is not the difference — a template can be extremely detailed
and still have decided nothing.

---

## Exercise 8 — Seven documents as one connected system

**Task.** Describe the seven Harrismith source documents as **one connected
management system**. Not a list — a system, in which each document has a job the
others do not.

**Why this exercise.** If it comes out as a list, the audience hears seven
documents. If it comes out as a system, they hear one agreement with its detail
organised.

**What good looks like:** the BEP at the centre stating what is required; the
matrices, schedule and strategies holding detail; the register recording
decisions, assumptions and open questions. And the reason for the split:
duplication creates divergence, and two copies of a rule both look
authoritative.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I name all seven and give each a distinct job?
- [ ] Did I explain *why* it is split, not just *that* it is?
- [ ] Did I place the register alongside the set rather than under the BEP?
- [ ] Did I avoid implying all seven are complete or operational?

**Common failure:** reciting the seven filenames. Nobody remembers filenames;
they remember that one document says what is required and the others say how it
is allocated.

---

## Exercise 9 — The two matrices, one sentence each

**Task.** Explain each responsibility matrix in **one sentence**. Two sentences
in total.

**Why this exercise.** Brevity forces the distinction. If you need a paragraph
each, you have not separated them.

**What good looks like:** something close to — *the first says who performs,
checks and authorises each step of the process; the second says which task team
produces and maintains each model.*

**Self-check**

- [ ] Exactly one sentence each?
- [ ] Is the difference audible, or do both sentences sound the same?
- [ ] Did I avoid RACI vocabulary?
- [ ] Could I follow up with one concrete example from each if asked?

**Common failure:** both sentences containing "who is responsible for". They are
both responsibility matrices — saying so distinguishes nothing. One is about
**process functions**, the other about **containers**.

---

## Exercise 10 — A delivery schedule, explained to a project owner

**Task.** Explain what an information-delivery schedule is, and why it matters,
to a project owner. Two minutes.

**Why this exercise.** The owner cares about commitment and risk, not fields and
identifiers. This is the same audience as Exercise 2, one layer deeper.

**What good looks like:** you explain that it converts "who is responsible for
this" into "this is due, to this person, for this reason, checked by this point,
authorised by this function" — and that this is what makes it a commitment rather
than an intention.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I explain purpose and recipient, not just content?
- [ ] Did I mention checking and authorisation as part of what makes it real?
- [ ] Did I avoid reciting the sixteen field names?
- [ ] Did I handle the absence of dates honestly rather than skipping it?

**Common failure:** describing it as a programme. It is not a programme, and on
Harrismith it contains no dates at all.

---

## Exercise 11 — Source-derived or teaching synthesis?

**Task.** From Slides 4–8, identify **one statement that is source-derived** and
**one that is teaching synthesis**. Say how you know.

**Why this exercise.** The credibility of the whole presentation rests on not
attributing your framing to the documents. This is the habit that prevents it.

**What good looks like:** you can point to the source for the first — a section
reference, or the document's own purpose statement — and you can say plainly of
the second that it is your explanation, not theirs.

**Worked answers available:** [`source-map.md`](source-map.md) section 5
classifies every significant Slide 4–8 statement. Do the exercise first, then
check.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Could I name the document and section for the source-derived statement?
- [ ] Did I resist the urge to justify the synthesis statement as "basically in there"?
- [ ] Do I know which of Slides 4–8 is *entirely* teaching synthesis? (Slide 5's comparison.)

**Common failure:** treating a reasonable inference as source wording. "The BEP
says a template isn't enough" — it does not. It says nothing about templates.

---

## Exercise 12 — "Who approves the BEP?"

**Task.** Answer the question **without inventing a named person**.

**Why this exercise.** This question arrives in almost every session, and the
pressure to produce a name is real. A fabricated approver is worse than an
admitted gap, because it manufactures authority nobody agreed.

**What good looks like:** you distinguish the *function* from the *holder*. On
Harrismith a defined approval function approved the training baseline with
conditions, **no real contractual signatory is assigned**, and every role holder
is TBD. On a real project the approver follows the appointment.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I answer with a function rather than a person?
- [ ] Did I say plainly that no holder is established?
- [ ] Did I avoid implying the BIM Manager approves their own work?
- [ ] Did I note that Harrismith's approval is *with conditions* and that publication is not authorised?

**Common failure:** "the client approves it." Perhaps — but no appointing party
is established on Harrismith, so that is an invention, and a plausible-sounding
one is the most dangerous kind.

---

## Exercise 13 — "When is this information due?"

**Task.** Answer the question **without inventing a date**.

**Why this exercise.** The pressure here is stronger than in Exercise 12, because
a date feels harmless. It is not — an invented date gets planned against.

**What good looks like:** you explain that Harrismith has **no established
milestones and no contractual programme**, that timing is **event-triggered or
TBD**, and that this follows from having no appointment and no client information
requirements. Then you say what *is* defined: the trigger, the purpose, the
recipient, the checking and the authorisation.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I get through it without a month, stage, quarter or duration?
- [ ] Did I explain *why* there is no date, not just that there isn't?
- [ ] Did I redirect to what a row does define?
- [ ] Did I resist "probably around design development"?

**Common failure:** offering a "typical" date as illustration. There is no such
thing as an illustrative date; the audience will write it down.

# Exercises for Slides 9–14

The final seven minutes: CDE states, folder-versus-status, the coordination
cycle, issue closure, approval versus implementation, and the Triviron transfer.

Exercises 22 and 23 are the full-run rehearsals and should be attempted only
after the rest.

---

## Exercise 14 — The CDE states, without saying "folder"

**Task.** Explain Harrismith's four CDE states **without describing them as
folders** — no "folder", "directory", "location", "where it sits" or "move it
to".

**Why this exercise.** The folder metaphor is the default, and it destroys the
concept. Removing it forces you to describe permitted use, which is what a state
actually is.

**What good looks like:** each state described by what the information may be
used for and who controls it. WIP — the team's own, not for general reliance.
Shared — available beyond the originating team, for a stated purpose, after check
and authorisation. Published / Authorised — authorised for a defined delivery or
use. **Record / Retained** — kept as evidence for traceability.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I get through all four without a location word?
- [ ] Did I say **Record / Retained**, not "Archived"?
- [ ] Did I say what each state *permits*, not where it lives?
- [ ] Did I mention that movement between them requires a decision?

**Common failure:** describing the states in order as a pipeline files travel
along. It is a sequence of permissions, not a conveyor belt.

---

## Exercise 15 — "Why can't I just move the file into Published?"

**Task.** Answer the question, in under a minute, to someone who has write access
to that folder and is mildly irritated.

**Why this exercise.** It is the most likely real challenge to Slides 9 and 10,
and it arrives with social pressure attached.

**What good looks like:** you separate the technical action from the governed
decision without implying the person is doing something wrong. Moving it changes
where it is; the state changes when the delivery review happens and someone with
the authority authorises it. On Harrismith that authority is **unresolved**, so
the transition is recorded as **blocked** — information stays Shared.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I distinguish the action from the decision?
- [ ] Did I avoid sounding like I was accusing them of anything?
- [ ] Did I mention that on Harrismith the authority is unresolved, not just unassigned to them?
- [ ] Did I resist inventing who *would* hold it?

**Common failure:** answering "because you're not allowed to." True in effect,
useless as teaching, and it makes governance sound like a permissions problem.

---

## Exercise 16 — Why permissions do not create authority

**Task.** Explain why CDE permissions do not create governance authority.

**Why this exercise.** This is the conceptual core of Slide 10 and one of the
most-repeated principles across the Harrismith sources.

**What good looks like:** platform access confers the technical ability to
perform an action, and nothing more — not authority to share, publish, approve or
accept. Access is configured to *follow* approved responsibility: responsibility
comes first, permission after. Where the two diverge, that is recorded as a
deviation rather than treated as a redefinition of who is responsible. And **CDE
Administration implements governance; it does not create it.**

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I state the direction — responsibility first, permission follows?
- [ ] Did I name at least two authorities that access does *not* confer?
- [ ] Did I say what happens when access and responsibility diverge?
- [ ] Did I avoid making it sound like an IT policy?

**Common failure:** arguing it as a security matter. It is not about restricting
access; it is about where authority comes from.

---

## Exercise 17 — The coordination cycle in under 60 seconds

**Task.** Describe the coordination-review cycle in **less than 60 seconds**.

**Why this exercise.** Slide 11 has roughly a minute. If the cycle takes you two,
the slide fails.

**What good looks like:** controlled Shared inputs → readiness check → federate →
check → findings → triage → assign the ones that need it → the originating team
corrects in its own WIP, checks, reshares under control → re-coordinate → verify
→ close with evidence.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Under 60 seconds?
- [ ] Did I include triage as a decision, not an automatic step?
- [ ] Did I say the correction happens in the originating team's own WIP?
- [ ] Did I include verification *and* evidence retention?
- [ ] Did I avoid naming a software product?

**Common failure:** starting at "run the clash test". The cycle starts with
controlled inputs and a readiness check, and skipping those is how coordination
runs on information nobody authorised.

---

## Exercise 18 — Recording, response, verification, closure

**Task.** Distinguish the four, in one sentence each.

**Why this exercise.** They collapse into "we logged it and fixed it", which
hides the two steps that actually make coordination reliable.

**What good looks like:** *Recording* creates a governed action record — a
decision taken at triage, not an automatic consequence of detection. *Response*
is the technical work, performed by the originating task team in its own WIP.
*Verification* confirms, after controlled reshare and re-coordination, that the
matter no longer exists or has an approved recorded disposition. *Closure*
follows verification against reshared information, with evidence retained.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Four distinct sentences, no overlap?
- [ ] Did I say who holds each?
- [ ] Did I make clear that closure follows re-coordination, not someone's word?
- [ ] Did I avoid implying the coordinator designs the fix?

**Common failure:** treating verification and closure as one step. Verification
is a check; closure is a disposition, and a material issue can fail verification
and stay open.

---

## Exercise 19 — "Does closing the ACC issue approve the design?"

**Task.** Answer it. Thirty seconds.

**Why this exercise.** It is the highest-consequence misconception in the module.
Someone acting on a closed issue as if it were design approval is a real project
risk, not a teaching nicety.

**What good looks like:** a clear no. Verification confirms that the coordination
process reached a disposition. It is expressly **not** design approval,
professional certification, publication authority or recipient acceptance. The
originating task team retains technical responsibility throughout — before and
after coordination.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I answer no, plainly, before qualifying?
- [ ] Did I name at least three things it is *not*?
- [ ] Did I say who retains technical responsibility?
- [ ] Did I avoid implying the platform decides anything?

**Common failure:** "well, sort of, it depends." It does not depend. The sources
are unambiguous, and hedging here teaches the wrong instinct.

---

## Exercise 20 — Approval versus implementation, with one example

**Task.** Explain the difference using **one** Harrismith example. Ninety
seconds.

**Why this exercise.** Slide 13 is the module's most valuable minute and a half.
One example, well told, beats the full unresolved list.

**What good looks like:** UD-001 is the strongest choice — observed, recorded,
confirmed current, and stopped at *decide intended state* with every downstream
step unreachable. Or the approval decision itself: approved with conditions,
explicitly not implemented as a live workflow, not verified through a governed
coordination cycle, with unresolved matters carried past approval.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I use exactly one example rather than listing everything?
- [ ] Did I distinguish approved / implemented / verified as three states?
- [ ] Did I present the incompleteness as discipline rather than failure?
- [ ] Did I stay out of publication-planning history?
- [ ] Did I avoid inventing when implementation might happen?

**Common failure:** drifting into the publication conditions. They are recorded,
they are interesting, and they are not this slide.

---

## Exercise 21 — Three forms of implementation evidence

**Task.** Name three things that would demonstrate a BEP is being implemented —
as distinct from approved.

**Why this exercise.** If you cannot name the evidence, the distinction is
rhetorical.

**What good looks like:** any three of — checking records; authorisation records;
state-transition evidence; coordination input, finding and Issue records;
verification records; transmission, receipt and acceptance records; retained
audit evidence.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Are all three *evidence of acts*, not documents describing intentions?
- [ ] Did I avoid offering "the BEP exists" as evidence?
- [ ] Could I say which function produces each?
- [ ] If I named correctly-named information, did I flag that no naming standard is established on Harrismith?

**Common failure:** naming approval artefacts — a signed decision, a status
designation. Those are evidence of approval, which is the other column.

---

## Exercise 22 — Close with three Triviron transfer questions

**Task.** Close the presentation using **three** transfer questions. Forty-five
seconds.

**Why this exercise.** Thirteen questions is a reference list; three is a
closing. Choosing which three is the exercise.

**What good looks like:** three questions that between them cover requirements,
authority and evidence — for instance: *what information does the client actually
require?*, *who authorises publication and who accepts delivery?*, and *what
evidence will show we are implementing it?* Then the closing position: Harrismith
gives a tested structure and a set of questions, not a completed BEP.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Exactly three?
- [ ] Did I assert any Triviron fact? (There are none in the repository.)
- [ ] Did I close on a position rather than trailing off?
- [ ] Did I avoid "we'll adapt Harrismith"?

**Common failure:** answering one of your own questions to sound conclusive. A
plausible answer given here will be quoted back as a decision.

## Exercise 23 — The final seven minutes, end to end

**Task.** Deliver Slides 9–14 straight through. Target **seven minutes**.

**Timing targets:** Slides 9–10 together 2 min; Slides 11–12 together 2 min;
Slide 13 1.5 min; Slide 14 1.5 min.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Seven minutes ± 45 seconds?
- [ ] Did Slides 10 and 12 survive, or did 9 and 11 eat their time?
- [ ] Did Slides 13 and 14 get their full three minutes?
- [ ] Did I say **Record / Retained**, and never "Archived"?
- [ ] Did I show or state that the publication transition is blocked?
- [ ] Did I invent any date, holder, or Triviron fact under time pressure?

**Common failure:** overrunning on the coordination cycle and arriving at Slide
13 with a minute left. Slides 13 and 14 are the payoff; protect them by cutting
Slide 11, not by rushing them.

**Self-check**

- [ ] Did I state the three honesty positions — training implementation; approved with conditions and not published; agreement is not implementation?
- [ ] Did I speak from the notes' essential messages rather than reciting wording?
- [ ] Could I identify, afterwards, which claims were source-derived and which were mine?
